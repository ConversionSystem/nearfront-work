Multi-location dispensaries and location pages

By Guillermo Bravo · September 8, 2026 · 12 min read · ← Dispensary SEO service

Multi-location work gets sold as one thing and is really two, with different rules, different failure modes and different people responsible for them.

The first is the profiles: a documented bulk process with a threshold, an account structure and a short list of rules that dispensary groups break in the same two places every time. The second is the location pages on your own site, which are the closest thing on a cannabis website to Google's published definition of doorway abuse. Neither problem is hard once you have read what Google actually says about it. Both are expensive when nobody has.

Ten locations is where bulk management starts. An agency holding several dispensary clients in one account cannot bulk verify any of them. A blank cell in the upload sheet is an invitation for Google to fill that field. And a set of location pages that differ only in the city name matches two of Google's four published examples of doorway abuse.

The profile side: what changes at ten locations

Google's bulk location management documentation is explicit about the threshold: "If your business has 10 or more locations you can add, verify, and manage them in bulk." Below that, each profile is verified on its own.

The counting rule catches people out. "Duplicate, suspended, or disabled profiles don't count towards the 10 Business Profile minimum." A group with eleven stores where two profiles are suspended has nine eligible profiles and no route to bulk verification until those are resolved.

Then the rule that decides how a chain should be structured, and the one almost nobody in this category mentions:

Your spreadsheet has 10 or more profiles from the same business. Agencies with more than one business in one account can't bulk verify.

Google Business Profile Help, Verify Business Profiles in bulk, read September 8, 2026

An agency that has tidied five dispensary clients into one convenient account has disqualified every one of them from bulk verification. The arrangement Google's documentation points at is one account or one business group per business, which is also what its authorized-representative rules expect: a representative manages a profile with the owner's consent and transfers ownership back on request.

Three more from the same documents, each a live trap for a dispensary group:

  • Your profile count has to match reality. "The profiles added to your account must accurately reflect the size of your business. You may be required to provide evidence if your chain size differs from the number of profiles in your account." The sheet must also be complete: "Do not leave out any businesses."
  • Uploading is not asking. "Uploading a spreadsheet doesn't send a verification request." Locations appear in the dashboard and sit there unverified while everyone assumes the clock is running.
  • Claimed profiles drop out silently. Bulk verification covers eligible profiles "not claimed by someone else". A store still owned by a former agency or a previous license holder is quietly excluded.

The two fields chains get wrong

Google's page on bulk verification delays names the causes of delay, and two of them describe how most dispensary groups set up their listings on day one.

Use a local phone number instead of a call center number whenever possible. Use direct phone numbers and URLs to the business listed, rather than redirects.

Google Business Profile Help, Understand delays in bulk verification

One central number across forty profiles, and every profile pointing at the homepage. Both are the default in chain marketing and both are named here. Google reinforces the point in its eligibility guidelines: a profile's phone number and website "should always be the single, authoritative phone number and website for the business and be verifiable by the business owner".

The website half has a second consequence worth noticing. If each profile points at its own location page rather than the homepage, that location page becomes the destination for the traffic the profile earns, which is the only reason to have built it. Point every profile at the homepage and the location pages are orphaned from the exact audience they were made for.

Two smaller ones from the same page. Business names must not carry "any additional information about the business, such as its specialty or store code", so store codes belong in the store code column of the sheet rather than in the visible name. And email addresses that do not use the business domain "will require additional steps toward verification and may take longer to process", which is worth knowing before a chain verifies from an agency address.

The blank cell that is not blank

This one deserves its own section because it scales badly and nobody publishes it. From Google's guidance on Google updates on your Business Profile:

If a field stays the same as your last upload, Google ignores it. If you change a field, the system accepts the new value: this new value replaces both the previous value and any updated info. If a field is blank: an automatic update may apply.

Google Business Profile Help, Understand Google updates on your Business Profile

A blank cell is not neutral. It is permission. Across forty locations, an empty column is forty openings for Google to fill that field from the third-party data and user contributions it says it compiles profiles from. If you have ever watched a chain's hours or categories drift apart over a quarter, this is one documented way it happens. The citations guide covers the wider mechanism.

The location pages: read Google's definition before you build them

Now the second problem, and the reason this page exists. A dispensary group with fifteen stores wants fifteen location pages. That instinct is right. The execution is where sites get into trouble, because Google has published exactly what the bad version looks like:

Doorway abuse is when sites or pages are created to rank for specific, similar search queries. They lead users to intermediate pages that aren't as useful as the final destination. Examples of doorway abuse include: Having multiple websites with slight variations to the URL and home page to maximize their reach for any specific query; Having multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page; Generating pages to funnel visitors into the actual usable or relevant portion of a site; Creating substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy.

Google Search Central, Spam policies for Google web search, read September 8, 2026

Two of those four describe a template-filled location-page set precisely. Pages targeted at specific cities that funnel users to one page: that is fifteen pages whose only real call to action is the same central contact form. Substantially similar pages closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy: that is fifteen pages differing in a city name, with no store locator anyone would actually use.

There is a second policy in the neighborhood, and it is about output rather than tools:

Scaled content abuse is when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users. This abusive practice is typically focused on creating large amounts of unoriginal content that provides little to no value to users, no matter how it's created.

Google Search Central, Spam policies for Google web search

Read "no matter how it's created" carefully, in both directions. A hand-written set of near-identical location pages is caught by this policy. A carefully sourced set that used AI somewhere in its production is not automatically caught by it. Google is describing the result, not the keyboard.

Four tests that separate a location page from a doorway

Google publishes no similarity percentage and no page-count limit, so these are the questions its own wording implies rather than a scoring system it endorses.

  1. Does the page hold something only that store knows? Its actual hours, its parking, which highway exit, what the staff are good at, what sells there and not elsewhere. If every fact on the page came from the head office spreadsheet, it is a template with a city name in it.
  2. Is the page the destination or a funnel? Google's phrase is pages that "funnel users to one page". If the only action is a form that goes to head office, you have built the thing described. A location page should let someone finish: hours, directions, the menu for that store, the phone number that rings that store.
  3. Is there a hierarchy a person would use? Google contrasts doorways with "a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy". A real store locator, states linking to cities, cities linking back, breadcrumbs that mean something.
  4. Would you publish it if search did not exist? The most useful test and the least technical. If the honest answer is no, the page was created to rank for a query rather than to serve a customer, which is the first line of the doorway definition.

The guardrail we run on our own pages

We are not writing this from the sidelines. This site publishes 34 market guides under a shared URL pattern, which is the exact shape of thing the doorway policy describes, so it is only fair to show the gate we run them through.

  • One primary dataset per page. Every guide is built from one state agency's own file, parsed directly, with the source, the period it covers and the date we read it printed on the page.
  • A thesis no sibling can print. Seattle's guide is about 58 percent of the city's dispensary closures sitting in four ZIP codes. Columbia's is about a place with no municipal government whose four stores share one ZIP. Baltimore's is about a city that belongs to no county. None of those sentences works on another page.
  • An automated overlap gate. Our build script compares every sibling pair by eight-word overlap, warns at 10 percent and fails the build at 15. The worst pair across all 34 guides currently measures 5.8 percent, and the two newest pages measure 2.9 percent against each other. Those thresholds are ours, not Google's, and we would rather publish them than imply Google set them.
  • A hierarchy that works both ways. Hub to state to city, each linking back, with a four-level breadcrumb on city pages.
  • A refusal rule. A market we cannot isolate in an agency's own file does not get a page. Las Vegas has no guide for that reason, and the site says so in public rather than quietly shipping estimates.

You can check every one of those claims. The markets hub is the index, and any guide will show you the sourcing on its own face.

Structure, markup and the store that is two stores

Three practical items for the site itself, once the pages are worth having.

Markup goes per location, on that location's page. Google's structured data documentation says to define each local business location as a LocalBusiness type, and that the markup makes most sense on a page containing information about that business. Our schema guide covers what is required, which is less than most vendors claim.

A medical counter is a department, not a location. If one address runs a medical counter alongside a recreational floor, Google's department property expresses that, with the naming format store name plus department name.

The age gate applies to every one of these pages. If your site redirects visitors to a single consent page, Google states that this "will remove all but that one page from search results". Fifteen carefully built location pages behind that pattern are fifteen pages Googlebot never fetches. The website spoke covers how to build the gate so that does not happen, and on a multi-location site it is the first thing to check, not the last.

Suburb pages, and where they belong

A separate question that arrives with the same conversation: pages for suburbs a store serves but does not sit in. Those can work, and the same four tests decide it. Each suburb page needs its own sourced local material and a real relationship to the store, usually the drive, and two suburbs that cannot be written distinctly should not both get a page.

One rule we hold ourselves to: those pages belong on the client's own domain, never on ours. An agency hosting its clients' local pages on its own site is building the "multiple domain names or pages targeted at specific regions" pattern with extra steps. The only such page on nearfront.com is the Gresham demo, which exists to show the format.

The multi-location checklist

  1. Count your eligible profiles, excluding duplicates, suspended and disabled ones, and confirm you are actually at ten.
  2. One account or business group per business. If an agency holds several clients in one account, none of them can bulk verify.
  3. Give every location its own local phone number and its own location-page URL, not the call center and the homepage.
  4. Never leave a cell blank in the bulk sheet. Fill every column you care about, every time.
  5. Keep store codes out of business names, and put them in the store code column.
  6. Verify from a business-domain email address.
  7. Send the verification request, because uploading the sheet does not.
  8. Audit every location page against the four tests before you publish the set, not after.
  9. Build a store locator a person would use, linking both directions.
  10. Check the age gate on a location page and confirm it returns 200 rather than redirecting to a consent URL.

Frequently asked questions

How many locations do you need before Google lets you manage profiles in bulk?

Ten. Google's documentation says that if your business has 10 or more locations you can add, verify and manage them in bulk, and that duplicate, suspended or disabled profiles do not count toward that minimum. A chain with eleven profiles where two are suspended is below the threshold without realizing it.

Can an agency bulk verify several dispensary clients at once?

No. Google states that the spreadsheet must hold 10 or more profiles from the same business, and that agencies with more than one business in one account cannot bulk verify. Putting several dispensary clients into a single account disqualifies all of them. The fix is a separate account or business group per client, which is also the arrangement Google's authorized-representative rules point toward.

Should every dispensary location use the same phone number and website?

Google's guidance says no on both. It advises using a local phone number instead of a call center number wherever possible, and using direct phone numbers and URLs for the business listed rather than redirects, and it names both among the issues that delay bulk verification. Google separately states that a profile's phone number and website should be the single authoritative ones for that business and verifiable by the owner.

What happens if I leave a cell blank in the bulk upload spreadsheet?

Google may fill it for you. Its documentation says that if a field stays the same as your last upload it is ignored, that a changed field replaces both the previous value and any updated information, and that if a field is blank an automatic update may apply. At forty locations, a blank column is forty invitations for Google to populate that field from third-party data.

Are dispensary location pages considered doorway pages?

They can be, and Google publishes the definition rather than leaving it to interpretation. Doorway abuse covers pages targeted at specific regions or cities that funnel users to one page, and substantially similar pages that are closer to search results than a clearly defined, browseable hierarchy. A set of location pages that differ only in the city name and push everyone to one contact form matches both descriptions. A page carrying that location's own hours, staff, parking, stock and local detail, linked in a real hierarchy, does not.

Does using AI to write location pages break Google's rules?

Not by itself. Google's scaled content policy is about output rather than tooling: it covers many pages generated primarily to manipulate rankings and not help users, creating large amounts of unoriginal content with little value, and it says explicitly "no matter how it's created". Its examples name generative AI used to produce many pages without adding value. A hand-written set of near-identical location pages is caught by that policy and a carefully sourced AI-assisted set is not automatically caught.

How should structured data be handled across multiple dispensary locations?

One LocalBusiness entity per location, on that location's own page. Google says to define each local business location as a LocalBusiness type, and that markup makes most sense on a page containing information about that business. If a store runs a medical counter alongside a recreational floor, that is the department property rather than a second location, and Google gives the naming format as store name plus department name.

How many location pages is too many?

Google publishes no number, and anyone quoting you one is inventing it. The test in Google's wording is not volume but whether each page is useful in its own right and sits in a browseable hierarchy. Our own answer is a measurement rather than a limit: we compare every sibling page against every other by eight-word overlap and treat anything above ten percent as a warning and fifteen percent as a failure that blocks the build. Those thresholds are ours, not Google's.
