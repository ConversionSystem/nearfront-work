Dispensary Near Me: How Google's Map Pack Actually Ranks in 2026

By Guillermo Bravo · Published July 1, 2026 · Updated August 31, 2026 · 13 min read · ← Spoke 1: Business Profile

Type "dispensary near me" into your phone and look at what fills the screen: a map and, usually, three businesses. That block is the Map Pack, and for cannabis retail it is the most valuable piece of real estate on the internet. Google's advertising policy prohibits ads for substances that induce highs, so there is no buying your way onto that screen. You rank there or you do not exist there.

Most dispensary owners know this much. What most do not know is how the pack actually gets ordered, and how much of what they have been told about it comes from Google rather than from the industry that sells the service.

This post was first published on July 1, 2026 and rewritten on August 31, 2026 against Google's own documentation. Three claims in the original did not survive that check, including one about reviews that we had stated as fact. What changed, and why, is at the bottom of the page.

What Google actually publishes

Google has one page on local ranking, and it is shorter than the industry's version of it. Google says local results "are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity," then defines the third under the heading Prominence. Both words appear on the same page, which is worth noting only because most write-ups quote one and imply Google uses it exclusively.

Relevance is how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for. Distance refers to how far each business is from the customer who's searching. If a customer doesn't share where they are, Google uses what it knows about their location. Prominence means how well-known a business is. This factor's also based on info like how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have.

Google Business Profile Help, Tips to improve your local ranking on Google

Two things are worth pulling out of that before going further.

First, Google does not rank or weight the three factors against each other. There is no published "biggest factor." An earlier version of this page called the profile "the single biggest lever," which was our phrasing, not Google's, and it is gone.

Second, Google states flatly on the same page that "there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." For a dispensary that sentence lands twice: you cannot buy the ranking, and because of the ads policy you cannot buy the space beside it either.

Relevance: the profile does the work

Relevance is the factor most under your control, and it is almost entirely a Business Profile job. Google's stated approach is unglamorous: "Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results," and the instruction is to make the information as complete as possible, naming the address, hours, category and attributes specifically.

The field-by-field rules, including the ones that are specific to cannabis and the ones that can get a profile suspended, are covered in our sourced guide to Google Business Profile for dispensaries. The short version as it bears on ranking:

  • Categories: Google's instruction is the fewest that describe the core business, not the most. Read the options from your own profile, because Google does not publish its category list anywhere.
  • Every field completed: hours including special hours, service options, attributes, payment methods.
  • Photos: Google lists adding photos and videos as a way to improve local ranking. It publishes no count and no cadence, so any specific number you have been given, including the "ten or more" this page used to recommend, is someone's rule of thumb.
  • Posts: useful for freshness, and for a dispensary they are constrained. Google's restricted-content rules bar offers, prices, coupons and purchase links for regulated goods. Show the store, do not sell the product.
  • Products: not available to you. Profile product listings are governed by Google's Shopping policy, which prohibits recreational drugs, so a THC menu cannot live there.

Prominence: links and reviews, and one belief we cannot source

Google names exactly two inputs for prominence: "how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have." It adds that "more reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking."

So the documented picture is review count and rating, plus inbound links. Now the part where we correct ourselves.

The earlier version of this page said, as a statement of fact, that "Google weighs review recency and velocity, not just the average," and offered a specific figure: that a store earning fifteen fresh reviews a month would generally outrank a store with a larger frozen count. Neither of those is something Google publishes anywhere we can find, and the figure had no study behind it at all. The velocity idea is widely held across local SEO, it is plausible, and it may well be true. It is still a belief rather than a documented ranking factor, and this site's entire argument is that you should be able to check what we tell you.

What we can say with a source: Google ties local ranking to how many reviews you have and to positive ratings. What we cannot say with a source: that fresh reviews outrank old ones. Treat anyone selling you a velocity target as selling you a belief, including our previous self.

Citations deserve the same treatment. Google names links to your business, not directory listings, and does not publish NAP consistency as a ranking factor. What Google does say is that complete and accurate information helps a profile show up for relevant searches. Keeping your name, address and phone consistent across Weedmaps, Leafly, Yelp, Apple Maps and Bing is worth doing, because customers and data quality both benefit and because inconsistency is a genuine liability. It is a weaker and more honest claim than the one usually made for citation packages.

Distance: why your rank number is probably lying to you

Distance is the factor nobody can optimize and almost nobody measures properly. Because Google ranks partly on how far the searcher is from the business, your Map Pack position is not a number. It is a surface that changes as the customer moves.

A dispensary can hold first place within a mile of the front door and be invisible three miles out, in exactly the neighborhoods a delivery arm depends on. If you check your ranking by searching from inside your own store, you are measuring the one location where you are guaranteed to look best.

This is why we track Share of Local Voice across a geographic grid: dozens of measurement points spread across the trade area, each returning your position for each keyword. It turns "we rank fourth" into "we rank first downtown, sixth east of the highway, and the next win is over there." That grid is also the only way to see whether work you paid for actually moved anything, because a change that shows at your address may be invisible two miles away, and the reverse happens just as often.

Asking for reviews the way Google says to

Since reviews are one of the two documented prominence inputs, how you collect them matters. Google publishes both a sanctioned mechanism and a set of prohibitions, and most dispensary review tactics fall foul of the second.

Google's own guidance is to "ask customers to visit a Google link or scan a QR code," to reply to reviews, and to "value all reviews," adding that "a mix of positive and negative feedback often feels more trustworthy." That last line is Google arguing against chasing a spotless rating.

What is prohibited is equally clear. Incentives of any kind in exchange for a review, a revision or a removal are "strictly prohibited." You may not screen for happy customers before asking, may not pressure people to review while they are on the premises, and may not ask for specific content. One more that catches dispensaries: Google's guidance on replies says "be conversational, not promotional" and to avoid using a reply to offer deals or promotions, which is the same restriction that governs Posts.

Asking every customer, identically, with nothing attached, is permitted and is the whole game. Reviews get their own guide later in this playbook.

Where AI search actually fits

This section used to say that LocalBusiness schema and a clean site are "how you become quotable to the machines." Google's own guidance on optimizing for generative AI search says something different, and we would rather print Google's version.

On what helps, Google is specific, and it points at the profile:

Where appropriate, generative AI responses can include product listings, product information, and information about local businesses. Using products like Merchant Center and Google Business Profiles can help your products and services to be visible in both AI responses and other Google Search results.

Google Search Central, Optimizing for generative AI search

On what does not help, Google publishes a mythbusting list, and two entries on it are being actively sold to dispensaries right now:

  • Structured data. Google says it "isn't required for generative AI search, and there's no special schema.org markup you need to add," while recommending you keep it because it "helps with being eligible for rich results on Google Search." Schema is worth having, and Google's LocalBusiness documentation is clear about what it is for: knowledge panel detail, hours and departments on Search. It is not an AI visibility tactic.
  • llms.txt files. Google says it does not use them, and that maintaining one "will neither harm nor help your site's visibility or rankings in Google Search, as Google Search ignores them." Google adds it is fine to keep one for other systems that do read them. We publish one on this site for that reason, and we are telling you plainly that it does nothing for our Google rankings or yours.
  • Chunking, AI-specific rewriting, and chasing mentions. Google says there is no ideal page length, no need to write differently for AI, and that seeking inauthentic mentions "isn't as helpful as it might seem."

The practical read for a dispensary: the same Business Profile that wins the Map Pack is the asset Google names for AI visibility. There is no separate AI checklist to buy.

What Google does not publish

Everything below is a real question with no Google answer as of August 31, 2026. If a vendor states one of these as fact, ask for the source.

  • How the three factors are weighted. Google lists them and says it keeps algorithm details confidential.
  • Whether review recency or velocity is a ranking factor. Count and rating are documented. Recency is not.
  • Whether citation consistency is a ranking factor. Links are documented. Directory citations are not.
  • Any photo count, cadence or threshold. Photos are listed as helpful. No number is published.
  • How many results the pack contains. Usually three in practice, but Google documents no fixed count and the layout varies.

What we corrected in this update

Publishing a correction log on our own article is unusual, and it is the point. Three things in the July version did not survive a check against Google's documentation:

  1. Review recency and velocity, stated as fact. The original said Google weighs them and gave a "fifteen fresh reviews a month" example. Google publishes neither. Both are gone, and the belief is now labeled as a belief.
  2. "Ten or more real photos." A reasonable rule of thumb presented as a requirement. Google publishes no photo threshold.
  3. Schema as the route into AI answers. Google says structured data is not required for generative AI search and names the Business Profile instead. The section is rewritten around Google's wording.

Two smaller fixes came with them: the primary category is no longer named for you, because Google does not publish its category list, and the reference to keeping "products" current is gone, because a dispensary cannot list THC products on a profile at all. The same standard applies to our market guides and to the rankings we publish about ourselves: when a claim cannot be sourced, it comes off the page and the change gets said out loud.

Frequently asked questions

How does Google rank the Map Pack for "dispensary near me"?

Google publishes three factors. Relevance is how well a Business Profile matches what someone searched. Distance is how far each business is from the searcher, and where the searcher has not shared a location Google uses what it knows about their location. Prominence is how well known a business is, which Google ties to how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have. Google does not say how the three are weighted against each other, and states that it keeps algorithm details confidential.

Can dispensaries pay to appear in the Map Pack?

No, twice over. Google states plainly that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. Separately, Google Ads policy prohibits ads for substances that induce highs, so a dispensary cannot buy paid placement around the pack either. That combination is why local SEO carries so much weight for cannabis retail.

Do more reviews mean a better Map Pack position?

Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking, and names review count as part of prominence. What Google does not publish is any weighting for review recency or velocity. The idea that a steady trickle of fresh reviews beats a larger frozen count is a widely held industry belief that we cannot source to Google, and we no longer state it as fact on this page.

Why does my dispensary rank differently in different parts of the city?

Because distance is one of Google's three published ranking factors, your position changes as the searcher moves. A dispensary can rank first within a mile of the store and be invisible three miles out, where much of its delivery revenue should come from. This is why a single rank number measured at your own address tells you almost nothing, and why we measure Share of Local Voice across a grid of points instead.

Do citations and NAP consistency affect Map Pack ranking?

Google names two prominence inputs: how many websites link to your business, and how many reviews you have. It does not name directory citations or NAP consistency as a ranking factor. What Google does say is that businesses with complete and accurate information are more likely to show up in local results. Keeping your name, address and phone consistent is sound practice for customers and for data quality, but the stronger claim commonly made about citations is not something Google publishes.

Does schema markup help a dispensary appear in AI Overviews?

Google says structured data is not required for generative AI search and that there is no special schema.org markup to add for it, while recommending you keep using structured data because it helps with eligibility for rich results on Google Search. For local visibility in AI responses, the thing Google does name is the Business Profile: Google states that using Google Business Profiles can help your products and services be visible in both AI responses and other Google Search results.

Does an llms.txt file help my dispensary show up in AI answers?

Not in Google Search. Google states that it does not use llms.txt or similar AI text files, and that maintaining one will neither harm nor help your visibility or rankings in Google Search because Google Search ignores them. Google adds that it is fine to keep one for other services or systems that do use these files. We publish one on this site for that reason, and it is not part of how anything here ranks on Google.
