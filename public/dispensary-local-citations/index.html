Citations and NAP for dispensaries

By Guillermo Bravo · August 31, 2026 · 12 min read · ← Dispensary SEO service

Here is the concession this page has to make before it makes any argument at all: Google does not name citations as a ranking factor.

Google describes prominence as based on "info like how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have." Links and reviews. A citation, in the sense the local SEO industry uses the word, is a mention of your name, address and phone on another site, frequently with no link at all. That is not in Google's list, and no amount of confident vendor language puts it there.

We sell citation work. Opening a page about it by conceding the usual justification is not a sales tactic, it is the only way to get to the argument that actually holds, which is stronger and more specific than the one you have been sold. Google documents, in its own help pages, that it builds your Business Profile out of outside data, that it will change your profile when outside sources disagree with it, and that it can refuse your own corrections when it cannot confirm them. That is the case. It has nothing to do with ranking juice and everything to do with keeping control of your own listing.

The short version: bad data about your dispensary on the web can overwrite your profile, block you from fixing it for up to 30 days, and leave you with an inaccurate listing that Google says "might not show up for relevant searches." None of that requires citations to be a ranking signal.

Where your profile actually comes from

Most operators assume a Business Profile contains what they typed into it. Google says otherwise:

Information in profiles is compiled from a variety of sources: publicly-available information, such as crawled web content (e.g., information from a business' official website); licensed data from third parties; users who contribute factual information (such as addresses and phone numbers), and content (such as photos and reviews), including business owners who claim profiles through Google Business Profile; information based on Google's interactions with a local place or business.

Google Business Profile Help, Understand how Google sources & uses info in Business Profiles

Four sources, and what you typed is only part of one of them. Crawled web content and licensed third-party data are both in that list, in Google's own words. That is the documented route by which information about your dispensary that lives somewhere else can end up on the profile customers see.

Note what Google does not say: it does not name a single one of the third parties it licenses data from. Every published list of "the data aggregators that feed Google" is inference. We are not going to repeat one.

Google will change your profile when outside sources disagree with it

This is the part that turns a data-hygiene chore into something worth paying attention to. Google's page on profile updates is direct:

To help keep your Business Profile accurate, Google gathers info from different sources, like user reports and licensed content. If sources report that your profile info is incorrect or outdated, Google may update your profile.

Google Business Profile Help, Understand Google updates on your Business Profile

And the sentence right after it, flagged Important by Google: "You can't manage all Google updates through your Business Profile."

So a change you did not make, sourced from data you do not control, can land on your listing, and some of those changes you cannot simply undo from the dashboard. Google does give you a tell. In the profile editor, blue text marks information changed by Google, and a notification appears at the top of the page when it happens. Checking for blue text takes ten seconds and is the single most useful habit in this whole area.

The bulk-upload trap

If you run several stores through a spreadsheet, Google documents exactly what it does with each cell, and one of the three rules is a trap:

  • A field unchanged from your last upload is ignored.
  • A field you change takes the new value, replacing both the old value and any Google update.
  • A blank field: "an automatic update may apply."

An empty cell is not neutral. It is an invitation for Google to fill that field from third-party data. Multi-location operators who leave columns blank because "we will fill those in later" are handing those fields to whatever the web currently says.

Bad data can block you from fixing your own listing

The loop closes here, and this is the argument almost nobody makes because it requires reading a third Google page. On profile edits, Google says:

Edits usually take up to 10 minutes to review, but sometimes it can take up to 30 days. Not approved: Google might not approve changes if it can't confirm its accuracy.

Google Business Profile Help, Understand what happens to your Business Profile edits

Read that against the sources list. Google confirms accuracy using crawled web content and licensed data. So if the web broadly says your dispensary is at the old address, under the old phone number, or trading under the name you used before rebranding, your correction is the outlier, and Google may decline it. A rejected edit can take up to thirty days to come back around.

That is the real cost of inconsistent business data for a dispensary. Not a missing ranking signal. A listing you have partially lost the ability to control, at a moment when you cannot buy ads to route around it.

Three documented consequences, in sequence: outside data can overwrite your profile; you cannot manage all of those changes from the dashboard; and your own correction can be refused because the rest of the web contradicts it. Citation work is how you make sure the rest of the web agrees with you before you need it to.

And an inaccurate profile is a visibility problem

The last link in the chain is the one Google states plainly on its ranking page: "Businesses with complete and accurate info are more likely to show up in local search results. If your business info isn't accurate, your Business Profile might not show up for relevant searches in your area."

That is a visibility claim about accuracy, which is exactly what citation work protects, rather than a visibility claim about citations, which Google never makes. It is a narrower claim than the industry's, and unlike the industry's you can go and read it.

What Google does say about your name, address and phone

Two rules bear directly on NAP, and both come from pages about something else.

One profile per business. Google's representation guidelines say "there should only be one profile per business, as this can cause problems with how your information displays on Google Maps and Search." Duplicate suppression is the one piece of classic citation work Google explicitly asks for, and dispensaries accumulate duplicates easily through POS integrations, old agencies and menu platforms that create listings on your behalf.

One authoritative phone and website. From the guidance for agencies managing profiles: the phone number and website "should always be the single, authoritative phone number and website for the business and be verifiable by the business owner." That is Google's own version of NAP consistency, and note the standard it sets: verifiable. A directory listing carrying a different number is not just untidy, it contradicts the thing Google says must be singular.

Both rules are covered in more detail in the Business Profile guide.

The cannabis-specific part, and its honest limit

A dispensary's business data lives in places a coffee shop's does not: Weedmaps, Leafly, Dutchie-hosted menus, state license registries, delivery platforms, and the local directories everyone else uses. Those listings are worth keeping correct, and here is precisely how far we can justify that claim.

What we can say: they are public, crawlable pages carrying your name, address and phone, and crawled web content is a category Google names as a profile source. Customers use them directly. Wrong information on them is a live liability whether or not Google ever reads it, because a customer who drives to a closed address does not care about the mechanism.

What we cannot say: that Weedmaps or Leafly feed your Google Business Profile. Neither appears in any Google documentation we have read, and Google names no specific third-party source at all. Anyone who tells you a Weedmaps listing directly updates Google is asserting something they cannot show you. We are not going to be that vendor.

The state license registry deserves a special mention, because it is the one source with a plausible claim to being the most authoritative record of your business, and it is usually the one nobody checks. If your registry entry carries a different legal name or a different address than your storefront signage, that mismatch is the kind of contradiction that surfaces later, in a verification, an appeal, or a rejected edit.

What Google does not publish

  • Whether citations affect ranking. Links and reviews are named. Directory citations are not.
  • Which third parties Google licenses data from. None are named anywhere. Every list you have seen is inference.
  • Whether Weedmaps, Leafly or any other cannabis platform feeds Google. No statement either way.
  • How many citations a business needs. No number, no threshold, no benchmark.
  • Whether unlinked mentions count for anything. Google names websites that link to you. Unlinked mentions are not addressed.
  • How to reverse a Google update you cannot manage. Google says some updates cannot be managed from the profile and does not publish a path or a timeline for those.

The working checklist

  1. Open your profile editor and look for blue text. That is information Google changed from outside data. Do it first, and do it monthly.
  2. Write down the authoritative version of your name, address and phone, exactly as it appears on your storefront signage, and use nothing else anywhere.
  3. Check the state license registry against that version. It is the most authoritative record of your business and the one least likely to have been checked.
  4. Find and resolve duplicate profiles. Google's rule is one per business.
  5. Confirm the profile carries the single authoritative phone and website, not a tracking number and not a menu platform's URL.
  6. Correct the cannabis platforms customers actually use, on their own merits, without pretending you know they feed Google.
  7. Never leave a blank cell in a bulk upload. Blank invites an automatic update.
  8. Fix the web before you need to change the profile, because a correction that contradicts everything else Google can see is the one it may refuse.

Frequently asked questions

Are citations a Google ranking factor?

Not one that Google names. Google describes prominence as based on info like how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have. Links and reviews, not directory listings. A citation in the industry sense is a mention of a business name, address and phone, often with no link at all, and Google does not name that anywhere we can find. The sourced reason to do citation work is different: Google compiles and updates your Business Profile from outside data, so wrong data on the web is a risk to the profile itself.

Why does inconsistent business data actually hurt a dispensary?

Three documented consequences, none of which require citations to be a ranking factor. Google says it may update your profile if sources report your info is incorrect or outdated, and that you cannot manage all of those updates. Google says it might not approve your own edits if it cannot confirm their accuracy, and an edit review can take up to 30 days. And Google says that if your business info is not accurate, your profile might not show up for relevant searches in your area. Bad data can overwrite you, block your corrections, and cost you visibility.

Where does Google get the information on my Business Profile?

Google lists four sources: publicly available information such as crawled web content including a business's own website; licensed data from third parties; users who contribute factual information such as addresses and phone numbers, including owners who claim their profile; and information from Google's own interactions with the place. Google does not name which third parties it licenses data from, so any published list of the aggregators that feed Google is inference rather than documentation.

Do Weedmaps and Leafly listings feed Google?

Nobody can source an answer to this. Google names crawled web content and licensed third-party data as inputs without naming any specific site, and neither Weedmaps nor Leafly appears in any Google documentation we have read. They are public, crawlable pages carrying your name, address and phone, which is the category Google describes, but that is an inference and we are labeling it as one. Keep them accurate because customers use them and because inconsistency is a liability, not because we can prove Google reads them.

How many citations does a dispensary need?

Google publishes no number, no threshold and no benchmark, so any count sold to you as a target is a vendor's packaging rather than a Google requirement. The useful frame is coverage and correctness rather than volume: the places customers actually look for a dispensary, plus anywhere already carrying wrong information about you, since wrong data is the thing Google documents as a risk.

What is a Google update on my Business Profile?

It is a change Google made to your profile from outside information rather than from you. Google says that if sources report your info is incorrect or outdated it may update your profile, and warns that you cannot manage all Google updates through your Business Profile. In the profile editor, blue text marks information changed by Google, and you get a notification when it happens. Checking for blue text is the fastest way to see whether third-party data has rewritten your listing.

Can a competitor change my dispensary's listing?

Not directly, but the mechanism that lets anyone contribute is documented. Google lists users who contribute factual information such as addresses and phone numbers among its sources, and separately says that if sources report your info is incorrect it may update your profile. So the practical defense is not secrecy, it is monitoring: watch for Google updates in your profile editor and keep the rest of the web consistent enough that a bad report is contradicted by everything else Google can see.

Does leaving a field blank in a bulk upload matter?

Yes, and it is the trap most multi-location operators miss. Google says that in a spreadsheet upload, if a field stays the same as your last upload it is ignored, if you change a field the new value replaces both the previous value and any Google update, and if a field is blank an automatic update may apply. A blank cell is not neutral. It is an invitation for Google to fill that field from third-party data.
