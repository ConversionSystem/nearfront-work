The margin problem

What has happened to cannabis prices in Massachusetts?

They have collapsed. The Commission's own price series starts at $14.17 per gram in November 2018, the first full month of adult-use sales, and reads $3.87 in June 2026. That is a fall of roughly 73 percent in the price of the core product.

Every operator in the state is selling the same gram for a quarter of what it used to fetch, which means the arithmetic of the business has changed underneath everyone. Costs that were tolerable at $14 are not tolerable at $3.87, and the cost of getting a customer through the door is the one that scales with every single sale.

This is also why the usual answer, spend more on advertising, is unavailable here. Google Ads prohibits promoting THC products to United States consumers and Meta does the same. No Massachusetts dispensary can buy its way out of a margin problem, which leaves organic visibility as the only acquisition channel that compounds instead of recurring.

In short: Massachusetts adult-use cannabis fell from an average of $14.17 per gram in November 2018 to $3.87 in June 2026, roughly a 73 percent decline. Because Google and Meta both prohibit paid THC advertising, no operator can offset thinner margins by buying traffic, so organic local search is the only acquisition channel available.

Licensed is not open

How many dispensaries are actually trading in Massachusetts?

406 of 457 retail licenses have commenced operations. Fifty-one have been issued and are not yet trading. Massachusetts is one of the few states that publishes a commencement flag on each license, so this is a fact rather than an estimate.

That distinction matters when you size your competition. A license count tells you how crowded the market might eventually be; the commencement flag tells you how crowded it is this quarter. Anyone quoting 457 as the number of Massachusetts dispensaries is describing a market that does not exist yet.

Those 406 open stores are spread across 150 towns, and 64 of those towns contain exactly one. So the state has two quite different competitive realities inside it: a handful of dense municipalities, Boston with 37 and Worcester with 15, and a long tail of single-store towns where the nearest rival is somewhere else entirely.

In short: 406 of Massachusetts' 457 retail licenses have commenced operations, with 51 issued but not yet trading. The 406 open stores sit in 150 towns, and 64 of those towns have exactly one, so a dense-market strategy and a single-store-town strategy are both needed depending on where you are.

A gap in the state's own data

Do equity licensees open stores at the same rate as everyone else?

Not according to the Commission's file. Massachusetts built one of the earliest cannabis equity programs in the country, and its license data records which review track each applicant came through. Splitting retail licenses by that field shows a clear difference in how many reach the point of opening a door.

Of 218 retail licenses held by general applicants, 202 have commenced operations, or 93 percent. Of the 74 held through the Social Equity Participant and Economic Empowerment tracks, 52 have commenced, or 70 percent. Both numbers come from the same public file, counted the same way.

We report the gap and stop there, because the file does not say what causes it and we are not going to guess in public. What it does mean commercially is straightforward: a cohort of operators is reaching the market later and, by every account of cannabis financing, with less capital behind them. For an operator in that position, a channel that compounds rather than one that bills monthly is not a preference, it is the only thing that works.

In short: In the Commission's license file, 202 of 218 general-applicant retail licenses have commenced operations, or 93 percent, against 52 of 74 equity-track licenses, or 70 percent. Both counts come from the same public file. The data records the gap but not its cause.

Where the open stores are

Boston holds 37 open retailers, Worcester 15, and Northampton, Brockton and Cambridge 10 each, with Fall River, Holyoke, Pittsfield, Lynn and Somerville close behind. Beyond that the map is a long tail: 64 of the 150 towns with retail have exactly one store. Licenses, commencement status and the price series are all published by the Massachusetts Cannabis Control Commission open data catalog.
