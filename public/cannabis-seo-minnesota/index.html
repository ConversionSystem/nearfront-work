Two registries, one shelf

Who is actually selling cannabis products in Minnesota?

Two different populations, recorded on two different sheets of the same state file. The cannabis sheet holds 313 licensed businesses: 219 microbusinesses, 19 medical cannabis combination businesses, 17 mezzobusinesses, 14 standalone retail licenses, 12 transporters, 8 wholesalers, 7 cultivators, 6 testing facilities, 5 delivery services, 4 event organizers and 2 manufacturers. The hemp sheet holds 2,742 registrations, of which 2,611 are lower-potency hemp edible retailers.

That is roughly eight registered hemp retailers for every licensed cannabis business in the state, and they are not a fringe. Minnesota's 2022 hemp law let these products onto shelves years before licensing began, which is why the state collected $11.6 million of cannabis gross receipts tax in fiscal 2024, at the original 10 percent rate, before a single licensed dispensary existed.

For search, the practical consequence is that a Minnesota shopper's query does not distinguish between the two. Someone searching for gummies or THC near a suburb gets both populations mixed together in the results, and the licensed business has to earn its position against a much larger incumbent field that had a multi-year head start on reviews and local signals.

In short: Minnesota's state file lists 313 licensed cannabis businesses, 219 of them microbusinesses, alongside 2,611 registered lower-potency hemp edible retailers on a separate sheet. Hemp retailers outnumber licensed cannabis businesses roughly eight to one and predate licensing, having generated $11.6 million in cannabis gross receipts tax in fiscal 2024 before any dispensary opened.

The border pattern

Which Minnesota cities hold the most cannabis licenses?

Minneapolis leads with 24 and Saint Paul follows with 23, which surprises nobody. Third place does: Moorhead, with 15, more than twice Duluth's six. Winona and Albert Lea also hold six each, and Mankato and Burnsville five apiece.

Look at where those cities sit. Moorhead is on the North Dakota line. Winona sits on the Wisconsin border, Albert Lea near the Iowa one. The state publishes the license addresses but nothing about where customers come from, so we will not assert a cause. What is verifiable is that license density in Minnesota is not simply following population, and three of the state's most licensed cities are on state lines.

If you are planning a Minnesota footprint, that pattern is worth more than a population ranking. It suggests the competitive question in a border city is not how many people live there, and the operators already licensed in Moorhead have made a bet that a market-size spreadsheet would not have produced.

In short: By licensed cannabis businesses: Minneapolis 24, Saint Paul 23, Moorhead 15, then Winona, Duluth and Albert Lea with six each and Mankato and Burnsville with five. Moorhead sits on the North Dakota border and holds more than twice Duluth's count. Minnesota publishes license addresses but no customer-origin data.

What a microbusiness market means

What does a market of 219 microbusinesses look like?

It looks like a lot of small operators with small budgets, which changes what competing well requires. Microbusinesses are Minnesota's low-capital tier and they make up 70 percent of every cannabis license the state has issued. The field is not a handful of well-funded chains dividing the map; it is hundreds of independent businesses, most of them opening within months of each other.

Nobody in that field has an entrenched search position, because nobody has had time to build one. Equally, nobody has a marketing department. The operator who runs review generation weekly and keeps a complete, accurate profile is competing against businesses doing neither, at the exact moment when Google is deciding which Minnesota cannabis businesses it trusts.

The state's aid history makes the timing concrete. Minnesota's 2025 Local Government Cannabis Aid paid cities nothing, because the cannabis office certified zero licensed businesses as of 1 January 2025 and the aid was then repealed. Every licensed cannabis business in this state is younger than that certification, which means the assets that decide the next decade of local search are being built right now.

In short: 219 of Minnesota's 313 cannabis licenses are microbusinesses, the low-capital tier, so the field is hundreds of small independents rather than a few funded chains. None has an established search position: the state certified zero licensed businesses as of 1 January 2025, so every operator's local signals are under two years old.

FAQ

Common questions about cannabis SEO in Minnesota

How many licensed cannabis businesses does Minnesota have?

313 as of the state's licensed business file dated 6 July 2026: 219 microbusinesses, 19 medical cannabis combination businesses, 17 mezzobusinesses, 14 retail, 12 transporters, 8 wholesalers, 7 cultivators, 6 testing facilities, 5 delivery services, 4 event organizers and 2 manufacturers.

How many hemp retailers are there in Minnesota?

The same state file lists 2,742 lower-potency hemp registrations, of which 2,611 are hemp edible retailers, plus 67 manufacturers and 64 wholesalers. They are a separate registration from a cannabis license and should never be added to the 313.

Which Minnesota city has the most cannabis licenses?

Minneapolis with 24, then Saint Paul with 23 and Moorhead with 15. Winona, Duluth and Albert Lea hold six each, Mankato and Burnsville five each.

When did cannabis licensing start in Minnesota?

The cannabis office certified zero licensed cannabis businesses as of 1 January 2025, and the first license in the state roster is dated 18 June 2025. Adult-use was legalized in 2023, but licensed businesses are a 2025 development. Lower-potency hemp edibles were on shelves earlier under the 2022 law.

What taxes apply to cannabis in Minnesota?

A 15 percent cannabis gross receipts tax plus the 6.875 percent state sales tax and any local taxes, per the Department of Revenue. The original 2023 rate was 10 percent, and collections of $11.6 million in fiscal 2024 at that rate came entirely from the hemp-derived market.
