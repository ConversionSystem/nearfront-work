Google Business Profile for dispensaries

By Guillermo Bravo · Updated August 31, 2026 · 16 min read · ← Dispensary SEO service

Most guides to Google Business Profile for dispensaries are the generic local SEO checklist with the word "dispensary" pasted into the headings. Several of the pages ranking for this topic still call the product Google My Business, which is not what Google's own help center calls it. None of them quote the two Google policies that actually decide what a cannabis retailer can and cannot do with a profile.

This page does the opposite. Every rule below is quoted from Google's own documentation and linked to the page it came from, all of it read on August 31, 2026. Where Google does not publish something, and there is more of that than the rest of this field admits, the page says so instead of filling the gap with confident guessing. A whole section near the end is nothing but the unanswered questions.

The short version: a storefront dispensary is fully eligible for a Business Profile, a delivery-only cannabis operation is not, the products section is closed to you, promotional posts are restricted, and the field most likely to get you suspended is the one holding your business name.

Is a dispensary even eligible?

Start here, because the answer is more specific than most operators expect, and because Google names cannabis directly in it.

Google's baseline eligibility rule is about physical contact. To qualify for a Business Profile, Google requires that "a business must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours." A licensed dispensary with a retail floor satisfies this easily. The important consequence is in the second half of that sentence: your stated hours should be the hours the store is genuinely open to people, not the hours you process orders.

The cannabis-specific rule sits a little further down the same page, in the list of ineligible businesses:

Businesses associated with products or services that require the customer to be a certain minimum age, like alcohol, cannabis, or weapons, aren't permitted as service-area businesses without a storefront.

Google Business Profile Help, Business eligibility and ownership guidelines

This is the single most consequential cannabis-specific Business Profile rule Google publishes, and it is one of the very few places where Google names cannabis at all. A service-area business is one that travels to customers rather than serving them at an address. Read plainly, the rule says a delivery-only cannabis operation does not qualify for a Business Profile.

What it does not say is that a dispensary cannot deliver. A store with a retail location that also runs delivery is eligible on the strength of the storefront, because the storefront is where in-person contact happens. The rule bites when there is no storefront at all: a delivery brand operating from a warehouse or a non-public address has no eligible profile to build, and any profile it does create is exposed.

If that describes part of your operation, the honest planning conclusion is that local search visibility for the delivery arm has to come from your own website and the marketplaces, not from a map listing you are not entitled to.

Verification, and why you do not get to pick the method

Verification is where operators lose the most time, usually by trying to choose a method. You cannot. Google's verification page carries this as an "Important" note:

Verification methods are automatically determined by Google and can't be changed.

Google Business Profile Help, Verify your business on Google

The same page says the options available depend on "your business type, public info, region, or business hours," and that in some cases more than one method is required. Google adds that where video verification is offered, it recommends taking it.

Two practical notes for a dispensary. First, if phone or text verification comes up, Google warns that "Interactive Voice Response (IVR) systems won't get the code." Multi-location operators whose published number reaches a phone menu will fail this method without understanding why. Second, live video verification is only available within your location's stated business hours, and Google asks you to show three things during the call: your location, your business or equipment, and proof you manage the place.

A dispensary is unusually well equipped for that call. Exterior signage and a street sign cover location. The license displayed on the wall, the POS terminals and branded packaging cover the business. Access to the secure storage room or the back of house covers management. Have all three ready before the call rather than walking around looking for them while a reviewer waits.

The name field is where dispensaries get suspended

If you audit one field, audit this one. Google states the risk in the same breath as the rule: "Including unnecessary information in your business name isn't permitted, and could result in the suspension of your Business Profile."

The guidelines say your name "should reflect your business's real-world name, as used consistently on your storefront, website, stationery, and as known to customers," and then list what may not be added. Google's own examples make the standard concrete: "TD Bank, America's Most Convenient Bank" is not permitted, "TD Bank" is. "Midas Auto Service Experts" is not permitted. Applied to a dispensary, these are the additions that break the rule:

  • Keywords describing what you sell. Google forbids "service or product information" in the name. Appending "Dispensary" to a store not actually called that, or "Cannabis Store," falls here.
  • Location keywords that are not part of the name. The city belongs in the address field, not the name.
  • Marketing taglines. "Best prices in Denver" is a tagline, not a name.
  • Store codes, such as a location number appended to a chain name.
  • Fully capitalized words, unless that is genuinely the brand's styling.
  • Hours, phone numbers or a URL.
  • Trademark and registered symbols.

There is one documented exception worth knowing, because it is commonly misreported as an absolute ban. Special characters and legal terms such as LLC or INC are permitted if you can show real-world proof: Google asks for "signage, business cards, invoices" that consistently display those elements as part of the name. The test is the real world, not the field.

The uncomfortable part of this rule is that keyword stuffing the name often works for a while. It is also the clearest documented path to losing the listing, on a profile that for a dispensary is close to irreplaceable, since you cannot buy ads to cover the gap while you appeal.

Categories: fewer, not more

Standard local SEO advice is to add every secondary category that could plausibly apply. Google's published instruction is the reverse:

Choose the fewest number of categories it takes to describe your overall core business.

Google Business Profile Help, Guidelines for representing your business on Google

The same section adds that "there should only be one profile per business," which matters for operators who have accumulated duplicates over the years through POS integrations, old agencies and franchise tooling.

Here is where we stop short of what competing guides will tell you. Several pages ranking for this topic publish a confident ranked list of the cannabis categories to select. We cannot verify that list against a Google source. Google does not publish its Business Profile category list anywhere in the Help Center, and the article that once described category management now returns a 404. We tried to confirm category names by reading a live dispensary profile in search results on August 31, 2026 and were blocked by Google's bot protection, which we do not work around.

So the sourced, honest instruction is procedural rather than prescriptive: open the category field in your own profile, type what you are, and read the options Google actually offers your business in your region. That list is authoritative for you in a way that a blog post's list is not, and it takes a minute. Then apply Google's rule and pick the fewest that describe the core business.

What you cannot post, and the one thing that is carved out

This is the section that separates a dispensary profile from a coffee shop's, and it is governed by Google's restricted content policy. The operative passage:

The content you upload may not feature calls to action or offers for the sale of products or services that are subject to local legal regulations. This includes, but is not limited to, alcohol, gambling, tobacco, guns, health and medical devices, regulated pharmaceuticals, adult services, and financial services.

Google Business Profile Help, Prohibited & restricted content

Read that list carefully, because being accurate about it matters. Cannabis is not named in it. Plenty of agency content asserts that it is. What the policy does say is "includes, but is not limited to," and the governing description is products "subject to controls and local regulations," which state-licensed cannabis plainly is. So the reasonable reading is that the rule reaches cannabis, while the honest statement is that Google has not written the word into this particular list. Both things are true and you deserve to know which is which.

The policy then gets specific about what the content must not display:

  • Links to a landing page where it is possible to purchase restricted goods or services.
  • An email address or phone number to contact for the purchase of restricted goods.
  • Promotional offers for restricted goods, and Google spells out the examples: "deals, coupons, pricing information or other promotions."

That reaches Posts and Photos, because Google's photos, videos and posts policy does not set a softer standard of its own. It states that "our prohibited and restricted content policies apply to your submitted or published photos and videos" and that you are responsible for content complying "with all applicable laws and regulations." So a Post advertising 20% off flower is squarely inside what the policy describes.

Now the carve-out, which is genuinely useful and almost never mentioned:

Note that incidental depictions of these products are excluded from this policy. Examples include: Images of menus.

Google Business Profile Help, Prohibited & restricted content

Google names menu images explicitly as an excluded incidental depiction. The distinction the policy draws is between depicting and offering. A photograph of your menu board is a depiction of your store. A Post reading "this weekend only, $99 ounces, order here" is an offer with a price and a call to action, which is what the rule addresses.

Useful line to hold in your head when planning Posts: show the store, do not sell the product. Store news, hours, a new location, an event, staff, the space, and photos of what the shop looks like are all normal profile content. Prices, discounts, coupons and order links are what the restricted-content rule speaks to.

The products section is closed to you, and it takes two documents to prove it

Operators keep asking whether the dispensary menu can live in the profile's Products section. It cannot, for THC products, and the reasoning takes two hops through Google's own policies.

First, Google's product approval policies establish which rulebook governs profile products: uploaded product data that "doesn't meet the product data specifications or Shopping policy requirements" is subject to "warnings, disapprovals, limited visibility or suspension."

Second, the Shopping ads policies list what is prohibited under dangerous products: "Recreational drugs (chemical or herbal); psychoactive substances; equipment to facilitate drug use."

Chain those together and a dispensary's THC catalogue is prohibited content under the policy that governs profile products. There is a second, independent limit on the same feature: Google notes that in-store product display "is available only for retail businesses in eligible countries or regions." Either one is enough to stop building a strategy around it. Put the menu on your own site instead, where you control the rules, which is a topic in its own right.

Reviews: what Google actually permits

Reviews get their own guide in this playbook, because for a dispensary they are both a ranking input and a compliance surface. The rules belong here too, because they live inside the same restricted content policy most operators never open.

Google lists four things merchants may not do. You may not offer incentives "such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services" in exchange for a review, a revision, or the removal of a negative one. You may not "discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers," which is the clause that outlaws review gating, the practice of screening for happy customers before asking. You may not "require or pressure users to leave ratings or write reviews while on the premises," which speaks directly to the budtender handing over a tablet at checkout. And you may not "request that specific content be included."

Google is equally explicit about what remains allowed, and it is more than nervous operators assume:

We do allow merchants to: Solicit or encourage the posting of content that does represent a genuine experience, without offering incentives to do so or attempting to influence the rating or the contents of the review.

Google Business Profile Help, Prohibited & restricted content

Asking every customer, in the same way, with nothing attached and no steer on what to write, is permitted. Asking only the happy ones, or attaching a discount, is not. That single distinction resolves most of the review tactics sold to dispensaries.

What Google says actually moves the ranking

Google publishes its local ranking factors, and the published version is shorter and less exciting than the industry's version of it. Google's page says local results "are mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity," then defines the third factor under the heading Prominence. Google uses both words on that page, which is worth noting only because most write-ups quote one and imply it is the only term used.

  • Relevance is "how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for." Google's stated answer is complete and detailed business information, which is what the categories, description, hours and attributes are for.
  • Distance is how far the business is from the searcher, and where the searcher has not shared a location, Google "uses what it knows about their location." This is the factor you cannot influence, and the reason a single measurement at your own address tells you very little about how you rank across a city.
  • Prominence is "how well-known a business is," and Google ties it to "how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have."

Google also states, flagged Important on its own page, that "there's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." Worth keeping to hand the next time someone offers to sell your store a Map Pack position as though it were inventory.

The practical consequence of distance being a real factor is that where you measure decides what you see. Ranking first when you stand in your own shop is close to meaningless; what matters is how far your visibility reaches into the neighborhoods your customers actually drive from. That is a measurement problem, covered in how the Map Pack ranks dispensaries.

If your profile gets suspended

Losing the profile is worse for a dispensary than for most retailers, because the usual emergency lever, buying ads while you sort it out, is not available to you. Google's appeals process is documented, and two details in it decide most outcomes.

Gather your documents before you open the form. Google warns: "Once you open the evidence form, you must submit it within 60 minutes or it won't be attached to your appeal." Operators routinely open it first and then go hunting for paperwork. Google lists what helps: official business registration, a business license, tax certificates, and utility bills such as electricity, phone, water or internet. For a dispensary the state cannabis license matches Google's "a business license" example exactly, and it is the strongest single document you have. Google adds that the business name and address on every document should match the profile you are appealing, which is worth checking before you upload rather than after.

Do not create a replacement profile. Google states it plainly: "Do not create a new Business Profile for the same business while your appeal is under review." Creating a duplicate is the instinctive response to a suspension and it works against you.

One expectation to set for after a successful appeal: if you had products on the profile, they do not come back instantly. Google says all products move to "Not Approved" on suspension and are reprocessed "within 5 days of reinstatement."

If an agency manages your profile

We are an agency writing this, so read the following as a standard to hold us to as much as anyone else. Google has a specific rulebook for what it calls authorized representatives, which is any company managing a profile it does not own. It is on the same eligibility page as the cannabis rule, and it is short enough to check your current vendor against.

An authorized representative must never claim a profile "without express consent from the business owner," must "always work directly with the business owner to complete verification," must keep the owner informed about what actions it will take, and must "always transfer Business Profile ownership to the business owner immediately upon request." Google adds that failure to follow these "may result in a suspension for the Business Profile and/or Google Account," so a vendor's shortcuts become your suspension.

One line in that section settles a long-running argument about call tracking:

Note that the phone number and website for a Business Profile should always be the single, authoritative phone number and website for the business and be verifiable by the business owner.

Google Business Profile Help, Business eligibility and ownership guidelines

That sits against dropping a tracking number into the profile's primary phone field. If call attribution matters to you, and it should, solve it without displacing the store's real published number.

Two more ownership details worth knowing. Google can revoke ownership if "you haven't signed in to your account recently," so periodic sign-in is a documented retention requirement rather than folklore. And a profile created through another Google product can lose ownership if that product's profile is suspended.

What Google does not tell you

This is the section other guides leave out, and leaving it out is what lets them sound more certain than the evidence allows. Everything below is a real question with no published Google answer as of August 31, 2026. If you see a page state one of these confidently, ask where the number came from.

  • The category list. Google publishes no Business Profile category list, and the help article that covered category management now 404s. Any ranked list of cannabis categories is someone's observation presented as documentation.
  • Whether Google enforces the restricted-content rule against cannabis specifically. The policy does not name cannabis, and Google publishes no enforcement guidance for the category. The rule's own language reaches it; the enforcement pattern is not documented.
  • How often dispensary profiles get suspended. No figure exists from Google or any regulator. Every suspension rate circulating in cannabis SEO content is unsourced.
  • Whether cannabis profiles face different verification treatment. Google says methods are assigned automatically based on business type, region, public information and hours, which leaves the question genuinely open rather than answered either way.
  • How long an appeal takes. Google says it reviews the appeal and emails a decision, with no stated turnaround. The only documented duration anywhere on that path is the five-day product reprocessing window after reinstatement.

We would rather hand you five honest unknowns than five confident inventions. If Google publishes an answer to any of these, this page gets updated and the change gets noted, which is the same standard we apply to our market guides and to our own rankings.

The working checklist

Everything above, in the order we would actually do it for a store:

  1. Confirm eligibility. Storefront with in-person customer contact during stated hours. If there is no storefront, stop and plan around the website and marketplaces instead.
  2. Audit the name field against Google's list. Strip keywords, taglines, store codes, hours and URLs. Keep special characters or legal terms only if signage and invoices back them.
  3. Reduce categories to the fewest that describe the core business, reading the options from your own profile rather than from a blog.
  4. Find and resolve duplicate profiles. Google's rule is one profile per business.
  5. Check the phone and website fields hold the store's single authoritative number and URL.
  6. Complete the rest of the information: full address, hours including special hours, and attributes.
  7. Rewrite the description without links of any kind, and without prices or promotions. Google forbids both outright.
  8. Prepare verification evidence before you trigger verification, and expect the method to be assigned rather than chosen.
  9. Set a Posts policy the team can follow: store news yes, priced offers no.
  10. Install one review request that goes to every customer identically, with no incentive and no screening.
  11. Keep an appeal pack ready: license, registration, tax certificate and a utility bill, names and addresses matching the profile. You will have 60 minutes when you need them.
  12. Measure across the trade area, not at your own address, because distance is a published ranking factor.

Frequently asked questions

Can a dispensary have a Google Business Profile?

Yes, if it has a storefront customers can visit. Google's eligibility rule is that a business must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours. Google separately states that businesses associated with products requiring a minimum customer age, naming alcohol, cannabis and weapons, are not permitted as service-area businesses without a storefront. So a licensed dispensary with a physical store is eligible, and a delivery-only cannabis operation with no storefront is not.

Can a delivery-only cannabis business get a Business Profile?

No. Google's business eligibility guidelines name cannabis directly and state that businesses associated with age-restricted products are not permitted as service-area businesses without a storefront. A service-area business is one that travels to customers instead of serving them at a location. If your operation has no storefront customers can visit, it does not qualify. A dispensary that has a storefront and also delivers is eligible on the strength of the storefront.

Can I put my dispensary menu in the Products section of my Business Profile?

Not for THC products. Google's product approval policies state that uploaded product data must meet Shopping policy requirements, and the Shopping ads policies list recreational drugs, chemical or herbal, along with psychoactive substances and equipment to facilitate drug use, as prohibited dangerous products. Google also notes that in-store product display is available only for retail businesses in eligible countries or regions, which is a second limit on the same feature.

Can a dispensary post deals or discounts on its Business Profile?

Google's restricted content policy says uploaded content may not feature calls to action or offers for the sale of products or services subject to local legal regulations, and specifically may not display promotional offers such as deals, coupons or pricing information, links to a page where restricted goods can be purchased, or contact details for purchasing them. Google's enumerated list of regulated goods does not name cannabis, but it is prefaced as not exhaustive and the governing test is whether the product is subject to controls and local regulations. Google does explicitly exclude incidental depictions, giving images of menus as an example.

Why do dispensary Business Profiles get suspended?

Google publishes no cannabis-specific suspension guidance and no data on how often cannabis profiles are suspended, so anyone quoting a rate is guessing. What Google does publish are the rules whose breach it ties to suspension: adding unnecessary information to the business name, operating as a service-area business when selling age-restricted products, and, for agencies, breaking the authorized representative rules. The name field is the one worth auditing first, because Google states plainly that including unnecessary information in your business name could result in suspension.

How do I get a suspended dispensary profile reinstated?

Use Google's Business Profile appeals tool, and gather your documents before you start. Google warns that once the evidence form is opened it must be submitted within 60 minutes or it will not attach to the appeal. Google lists official business registration, a business license, tax certificates and utility bills as evidence that strengthens an appeal, and a state cannabis license matches the business license example directly. Google also warns not to create a new profile for the same business while the appeal is under review. Google does not publish a typical decision turnaround.

How many categories should a dispensary choose?

Fewer than most advice suggests. Google's guideline is to choose the fewest number of categories it takes to describe your overall core business, which is the opposite of the common practice of adding every plausible secondary category. Google does not publish its category list anywhere, so the only reliable way to see the options available to you is to read what the category field offers inside your own profile.

Can I use a call tracking number on my dispensary Business Profile?

Google's guidance for authorized representatives states that the phone number and website for a Business Profile should always be the single, authoritative phone number and website for the business, and should be verifiable by the business owner. That rule sits directly against putting a tracking number in the primary phone field. If call attribution matters, solve it in a way that keeps the store's real published number as the profile's primary number.
