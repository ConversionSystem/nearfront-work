A roster that barely moves

How many dispensaries does Kansas City have?

Twenty-three, per the state's own licensee list, and the dates on those licenses tell the real story. Eighteen of the 23 received their approval to operate in 2021, during the medical-only era. Two more came in 2022. The adult-use launch in February 2023, the single biggest demand event in the market's history, added exactly zero new Kansas City dispensaries, because Missouri let existing medical licensees convert to comprehensive licenses rather than issuing a new round.

Since then the roster has grown by three: two approvals in December 2024 and one, Dream Leaf, in March 2025. That is the whole story of entry into the largest cannabis city in Missouri, five years, five new storefronts.

Statewide the picture is the same. Missouri's licensing is constitutionally capped, and the DHSS annual report shows just 7 new full dispensary licenses issued across the entire state in program year 2024. The 224 dispensaries on today's list are, to a first approximation, the market.

In short: Kansas City has 23 licensed dispensaries per the state licensee list, the most of any Missouri city. Eighteen were approved in 2021, two in 2022, none in 2023 when adult-use launched via license conversion, and three since, the newest in March 2025. Statewide, only 7 new full dispensary licenses were issued in PY24.

What a cap does to competition

What does a capped market mean for a Kansas City dispensary?

It removes the threat most operators plan around and replaces it with one most operators ignore. Nobody is opening across the street next quarter. But the flip side of frozen supply is that growth can only come from the demand side, taking trips from the other 22, and demand-side competition is fought almost entirely in search results.

The demand is real: Missouri did $1.26 billion in adult-use retail sales in program year 2024, with $2.2 billion cumulative in under two years of adult-use, per the DHSS annual report. That money splits across just 224 stores statewide. Every point of visibility share in Kansas City is worth more than it would be in an open-entry state, precisely because the denominator cannot grow.

The only meaningful new entry is happening at the edges: the state issued 25 microbusiness dispensary and 33 microbusiness wholesale licenses in PY24 under its social equity program. Watch that program, but the established field a Kansas City store fights today is the 22 other full licenses, most of them five years into their review histories.

In short: With entry capped, a Kansas City dispensary cannot lose to a new neighbor and cannot grow by waiting. Missouri's $1.26 billion PY24 adult-use market splits across a fixed 224 stores, so visibility share is market share.
