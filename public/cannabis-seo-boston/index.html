No chains

Who owns Boston's dispensaries?

Almost nobody owns more than one. Forty-six distinct businesses hold the city's 47 retail licenses, and the single exception holds two. For a city this size that is remarkable: there is no dominant operator, no regional chain running a dozen locations, and no central marketing department setting the pace.

It changes the competitive question entirely. In most metros the honest advice to an independent is that you will not out-rank the chain on broad terms and should not try. In Boston there is no chain, so the broad terms are genuinely winnable by whoever does the work properly. The ceiling on a single well-run store is much higher here than the same store's ceiling would be in Los Angeles or Houston.

It also means the standard of competition is uneven rather than uniformly high. A market of forty-six owner-operators contains some who maintain their listings meticulously and many who have not touched them since opening week. That spread is the opportunity.

In short: Forty-six separate businesses hold Boston's 47 retail licenses, with one operator holding two. There is no chain of any scale in the city, so broad search terms are winnable by an independent in a way they are not in consolidated metros.

Getting open is the hard part

Why do so many Boston licenses never open?

Ten of Boston's 47 retail licenses have not commenced operations. That is 21 percent, against 11 percent across Massachusetts as a whole. Whatever the causes, and the file does not record them, getting a licensed store trading in Boston is measurably harder than getting one trading elsewhere in the state.

For an operator already open, this is worth knowing for a practical reason: your competitive set is smaller than the license count suggests, and it is smaller by a wider margin here than anywhere else in Massachusetts. Someone sizing the Boston market at 47 stores is describing a city that does not exist.

It also means the market is still arriving. Ten licensed businesses are working toward opening, and each one will open into a city where search visibility is already spoken for or is not. The work done now decides which of those two it is.

In short: Ten of Boston's 47 retail licenses have not commenced operations, or 21 percent, against 11 percent statewide. The city's real competitive set is 37 trading stores, and the gap between licensed and open is wider in Boston than anywhere else in Massachusetts.

An unusual pipeline

What does Boston's license mix say about the market?

Boston is the only market we have measured where equity-track licenses outnumber general applicants. The city holds 13 Social Equity Participant licenses and 7 Economic Empowerment Priority licenses, twenty in total, against 18 general applicants, with the remainder on medical and diverse-business tracks.

The same commencement gap that shows statewide shows here, and slightly wider. Sixteen of Boston's 18 general-applicant licenses have opened, or 89 percent. Thirteen of the 20 equity-track licenses have opened, or 65 percent. Both figures come from the same public file, counted the same way, and the file records the difference without recording its cause.

The commercial reading is straightforward and worth saying plainly. The majority of Boston's licensed retail is held by operators who came through tracks designed for people without existing industry capital, in a category where no operator may buy paid advertising at all. For that group a channel that compounds over months, rather than one that bills every month and stops the day you stop paying, is not a preference. It is the only thing that works.

In short: Boston holds 20 equity-track retail licenses against 18 general applicants, the only market measured where equity licenses are the majority. Sixteen of 18 general-applicant licenses have opened, or 89 percent, against 13 of 20 equity-track licenses, or 65 percent.

Boston, for context

Boston is governed by the City of Boston, with cannabis licensing handled locally by the Boston Cannabis Board alongside state licensing from the Cannabis Control Commission. Retail runs through Dorchester, Roxbury, Jamaica Plain, Allston, the South End and Downtown, and Bostonians navigate by neighborhood rather than by address, which is exactly how they search.
